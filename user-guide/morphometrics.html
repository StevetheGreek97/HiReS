    Morphometric traits¶

    HiReS derives nine geometric descriptors from the polygon outline of every object. They correspond to the size and shape traits commonly used in ecological image analysis. Their names are listed, in order, in hires.TRAIT_NAMES.

    Trait Symbol Definition Range Unit Describes
    area \(A\) Enclosed area \(> 0\) length² Two-dimensional size
    perimeter \(P\) Boundary length \(> 0\) length Size and outline complexity
    solidity \(S\) \(A / A_\text{hull}\) \((0, 1]\) — Depth of concavities
    convexity \(K\) \(P_\text{hull} / P\) \((0, 1]\) — Smoothness of the outline
    circularity \(C\) \(4 \pi A / P^2\) \((0, 1]\) — Compactness
    bbox_width Extent along the image \(x\) axis \(> 0\) length
    bbox_height Extent along the image \(y\) axis \(> 0\) length
    obb_width \(W\) Short side of the oriented bounding box \(> 0\) length Body width
    obb_length \(L\) Long side of the oriented bounding box \(> 0\) length Body length

    Computing traits¶

    For any Shapely polygon, hires.measure returns the traits as a dict. A 4 × 2 rectangle:

    >>> from shapely.geometry import box
>>> import hires
>>> traits = hires.measure(box(0, 0, 4, 2))
>>> traits["area"], traits["perimeter"], traits["obb_length"], traits["obb_width"]
(8.0, 12.0, 4.0, 2.0)
>>> for name in hires.TRAIT_NAMES:
...     print(f"{name:12} {traits[name]:.3f}")
area         8.000
perimeter    12.000
solidity     1.000
convexity    1.000
circularity  0.698
bbox_width   4.000
bbox_height  2.000
obb_width    2.000
obb_length   4.000

    The shape descriptors tell shapes apart regardless of size. Compare the rectangle with a circle and with an L-shaped polygon:

    >>> from shapely.geometry import Point, Polygon
>>> shapes = {
...     "rectangle": box(0, 0, 4, 2),
...     "circle": Point(0, 0).buffer(1, quad_segs=64),
...     "L-shape": Polygon([(0, 0), (4, 0), (4, 1), (1, 1), (1, 4), (0, 4)]),
... }
>>> for label, polygon in shapes.items():
...     t = hires.measure(polygon)
...     print(f"{label:10} circularity={t['circularity']:.2f}  "
...           f"solidity={t['solidity']:.2f}  convexity={t['convexity']:.2f}")
rectangle  circularity=0.70  solidity=1.00  convexity=1.00
circle     circularity=1.00  solidity=1.00  convexity=1.00
L-shape    circularity=0.34  solidity=0.61  convexity=0.89

    The circle is the most compact shape (circularity 1); the L-shape has a deep concavity, which lowers its solidity.

    In practice you rarely call measure yourself. A collection measures all its annotations at once, in the right units:

    >>> collection = hires.Collection(
...     [
...         hires.Annotation(0, box(0.10, 0.10, 0.30, 0.20), confidence=0.9),
...         hires.Annotation(1, box(0.50, 0.50, 0.60, 0.90), confidence=0.7),
...     ],
...     name="scan",
...     image_size=(2000, 1000),
... )
>>> collection.to_frame()[["class_id", "area", "perimeter", "obb_length", "obb_width"]]
   class_id     area  perimeter  obb_length  obb_width
0         0  40000.0     1000.0       400.0      100.0
1         1  80000.0     1200.0       400.0      200.0

    and a single annotation is measured with Annotation.traits, given the image size:

    >>> collection[0].traits(collection.image_size)["obb_length"]
400.0

    Definitions¶

    Area¶

    The surface enclosed by the outline, computed from the vertices \((x_i, y_i)\), \(i = 1 \dots n\), with the shoelace formula:

    \[ A = \frac{1}{2} \left| \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( x_i \, y_{i+1} - x_{i+1} \, y_i \right) \right|, \qquad (x_{n+1}, y_{n+1}) = (x_1, y_1). \]

    Area is a direct estimate of body size that uses the full geometry of the object.

    Perimeter¶

    The length of the boundary, the sum of the Euclidean distances between consecutive vertices:

    \[ P = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sqrt{(x_{i+1} - x_i)^2 + (y_{i+1} - y_i)^2}. \]

    Perimeter reflects both size and boundary complexity. It depends on the resolution of the outline: a more finely sampled, more jagged polygon has a longer perimeter than a smooth one around the same object.

    Body length and width¶

    The oriented bounding box (OBB) is the rectangle of minimum area that encloses the polygon, at whatever angle achieves that minimum. With side lengths \(l_1\) and \(l_2\),

    \[ L = \max(l_1, l_2), \qquad W = \min(l_1, l_2). \]

    obb_length and obb_width are independent of how the organism is oriented on the scan, which makes them the appropriate measures of body length and width.

    Oriented versus axis-aligned

    bbox_width and bbox_height are the extents along the image axes. They depend on the orientation of the organism: for an elongated animal lying diagonally, both overestimate its width and underestimate its length. Use them for cropping, not for morphometry.

    Circularity¶

    \[ C = \frac{4 \pi A}{P^2} \]

    Equal to 1 for a circle and smaller for every other shape. It decreases as an object becomes more elongated or its outline more irregular.

    Convexity¶

    \[ K = \frac{P_\text{hull}}{P} \]

    where \(P_\text{hull}\) is the perimeter of the convex hull. Values near 1 indicate a smooth, convex outline; lower values indicate high-frequency irregularities such as spines or a jagged boundary.

    Solidity¶

    \[ S = \frac{A}{A_\text{hull}} \]

    where \(A_\text{hull}\) is the area of the convex hull, the smallest convex polygon enclosing the object. Low values indicate deep concavities or appendages.

    Convexity and solidity are complementary: convexity responds to roughness of the boundary, solidity to missing area.

    Units¶

    The traits are computed in whatever coordinate system the polygon is in. Which one that is depends on the collection:

    The collection has Lengths in Areas in
    no image_size normalized units normalized units²
    image_size pixels pixels²
    image_size, dpi and unit unit unit²

    The ratios solidity, convexity and circularity are dimensionless and do not change with scale.

    Always provide the image size

    In normalized coordinates a non-square image is squeezed into a square. area and solidity survive this (up to a constant factor), but every length, and with it perimeter, convexity, circularity and the bounding-box dimensions, is distorted. HiReS emits a HiresWarning when it has to measure this way. Load labels with image= or image_dir= to avoid it.

    Physical units¶

    A scanner resolution in dots per inch fixes the physical size of a pixel. For a unit with \(u\) units per inch,

    \[ s = \frac{u}{\text{dpi}} \]

    is the edge length of one pixel. Lengths are multiplied by \(s\), areas by \(s^2\).

    >>> scaled = collection.with_scale(dpi=2400, unit="um")
>>> round(scaled.pixel_size, 3)        # micrometres per pixel
10.583
>>> scaled.to_frame()[["class_id", "area", "obb_length", "obb_width"]].round(1)
   class_id       area  obb_length  obb_width
0         0  4480277.8      4233.3     1058.3
1         1  8960555.6      4233.3     2116.7

    The same objects as above, now in µm² and µm. The supported units and their number per inch:

    >>> hires.UNITS
{'nm': 25400000.0, 'um': 25400.0, 'mm': 25.4, 'cm': 2.54, 'm': 0.0254, 'inch': 1.0}

    The keys of hires.UNITS are what unit= accepts:

    unit Meaning Units per inch
    "nm" nanometre 25 400 000
    "um" micrometre 25 400
    "mm" millimetre 25.4
    "cm" centimetre 2.54
    "m" metre 0.0254
    "inch" inch 1

    dpi and unit must be given together, and the collection must know its image_size. Calling with_scale(None, None) removes the scale again.

    Resolution of your images

    The dpi must be the resolution at which the image was actually acquired. For a microscope, convert the calibration to dots per inch: a pixel size of \(p\) µm corresponds to \(25\,400 / p\) dpi.

    Edge cases¶

    • A polygon with zero perimeter or zero hull area yields 0.0 for the affected ratio rather than raising.
    • If no oriented bounding box can be constructed, obb_width and obb_length are nan.
    • For polygons with holes, area excludes the holes and perimeter includes the interior boundaries. Polygons read from label files never have holes.

    Validation¶

    HiReS was validated against manually annotated outlines of Daphnia pulex, Daphnia galeata and Simocephalus vetulus across nine full-resolution scanner images. The main findings were:

    • Automated and manual trait distributions preserved the same broad structure and relative ordering.
    • A consistent positive bias of 5–19 % was observed: a multiplicative scaling offset, not a distortion.
    • After centring by the median residual, agreement was near-perfect (\(r\) = 0.969–0.999).
    • Sample-level medians were strongly correlated (\(r\) = 0.94–0.96) despite the individual-level bias.
    • At low sampling depths (\(n\) = 10), automated medians outperformed manual subsamples in 43–62 % of cases.

    The positive bias likely reflects illumination-induced halos around the organisms that are partially included in the predicted mask. To quantify the agreement on your own data, see Evaluation.
